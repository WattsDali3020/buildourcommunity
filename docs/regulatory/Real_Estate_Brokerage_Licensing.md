# Regulatory Reference: Real Estate Brokerage Licensing Requirements
**State-Level Rules and Risks for Tokenized / Fractional Platforms**  
**For RevitaHub Tokenized Real Estate Platform**  
**Knowledge Base Entry – 4Horsemen**  
**Sourced: July 5, 2026**

This entry addresses state real estate brokerage licensing laws and the risk that a tokenized/fractional ownership platform (or its personnel) could be viewed as engaging in activities that require a real estate broker or salesperson license.

---

## 1. Core Issue

Most U.S. states have laws requiring individuals and entities to hold a real estate broker or salesperson license if they engage in certain activities for compensation, including:
- Listing, selling, buying, or leasing real property
- Negotiating real estate transactions
- Showing properties to prospective buyers/tenants
- Receiving compensation for referring or procuring real estate transactions

These are **state-specific** laws (unlike federal securities laws), and they can apply even if the offering is structured as a securities transaction.

---

## 2. Why This Matters for RevitaHub

A platform that tokenizes real estate interests could trigger brokerage licensing concerns in several scenarios:

### Higher-Risk Activities
- Actively marketing or promoting **specific properties** to investors
- Introducing investors directly to property owners/sponsors in exchange for fees
- Negotiating terms between investors and property sponsors
- Taking compensation that is tied to the closing or success of underlying real estate transactions
- Providing services that resemble traditional real estate brokerage (e.g., property tours, deal packaging, buyer representation)

### Lower-Risk Activities (Generally)
- Offering **securities** (tokenized interests) in SPVs that own real estate, where the platform’s role is limited to securities compliance, investor onboarding, and secondary market facilitation
- Purely passive holding and governance of tokenized interests after the initial offering
- Using properly licensed third-party real estate brokers where traditional brokerage services are needed

**Key Distinction**: Securities brokerage (already covered in prior entries) is regulated at the federal + FINRA level. Real estate brokerage is regulated at the **state** level and is a separate licensing regime.

---

## 3. Common State Law Triggers

While rules vary by state, common activities that can trigger licensing requirements include:
- Receiving any form of compensation (direct or indirect) for real estate-related referrals or transactions
- Holding oneself out as being in the business of buying, selling, or leasing real estate
- Negotiating or attempting to negotiate real estate transactions on behalf of others

Many states have broad definitions of “real estate broker” that can capture a wide range of activities if compensation is involved.

---

## 4. Platform Implications and Recommendations

### Structural Recommendations
- Clearly separate the **securities offering** function from any real estate services.
- Avoid compensation models that tie platform revenue directly to the success or closing of underlying real estate deals (as opposed to securities issuance or management fees).
- Use properly licensed real estate brokers or property managers for any activities that clearly require state licensing.
- Document the platform’s role as a securities issuer/platform rather than a real estate broker.

### Compliance Layer Considerations
- The KYC/AML and investor onboarding processes should be clearly framed around securities compliance rather than real estate transaction services.
- Marketing materials and user communications should avoid language that could be interpreted as offering real estate brokerage services.
- If the platform ever expands into property management, leasing, or transaction facilitation services, a licensing analysis should be conducted on a state-by-state basis.

### Secondary Market Considerations
Facilitating secondary trading of tokenized interests is primarily a **securities** activity (hence the broker-dealer partnership approach). However, if secondary transactions involve direct real estate conveyances or significant real estate services, additional state licensing analysis may be needed.
